Computer Networks Seminar

Bus Topology

Every device in the network is attached to one common cable, known as the backbone. All data moves along this line. Let us examine why this design is useful, and where its limits begin.

Scroll to begin ↓
Advantage 1 of 4
a

Easy Node Changes

Textbook lineNodes can be connected or removed easily from the bus network.

Each device joins the common backbone through a small connector, so a new computer needs only one drop cable to reach the bus, without affecting other devices. Removing a machine is equally simple, which makes this topology suitable for labs and classrooms where the number of systems keeps changing.

Advantage 2 of 4
b

Less Cabling, Lower Cost

Textbook lineIt requires shorter cable length than star topology.

Since all devices share one backbone instead of using a separate line to a central hub, the total cable required is much shorter than in a star layout. Shorter cabling reduces material cost and makes installation quicker and simpler, especially along a single row of desks.

Advantage 3 of 4
c

Easy to Install

Textbook lineBus network is easy to implement and can be extended up to a certain limit.

As no switches or hubs are needed, a technician can lay one cable, attach the drop cables, and bring the network into use, which suits small or temporary set-ups. Repeaters can extend it slightly further, but only until signal quality begins to decline.

Advantage 4 of 4
d

Ideal for Small Groups

Textbook lineIt works well for small networks.

In a small office or a single classroom, only a few devices share the cable, so competition for bandwidth is low and collisions remain rare. It is often the quickest and cheapest way to connect a handful of computers with one another.

Section Break

Every Design Has Its Limits

⚡

A single shared cable makes bus topology cheap and simple. That same cable is also its greatest weakness. Let us now see where it begins to fail.

Disadvantage 1 of 7
⚡
a

One Fault, Total Failure

Textbook lineIf there is a fault or break in the main cable, the entire network shuts down.

Every device relies on the same shared backbone to reach every other device, so a single cut or fault anywhere along that line breaks the path for all of them. No backup route exists, and the network stays down until the cable is repaired.

Disadvantage 2 of 7
b

Terminators Are Essential

Textbook lineTerminators are required at both ends of the backbone cable.

A signal that reaches the end of the cable has nowhere to go, so it reflects back, much like an echo, and collides with new signals. A terminator at each end absorbs the signal so that it fades out cleanly instead of reflecting back.

Disadvantage 3 of 7
c

Hard to Trace the Fault

Textbook lineFault isolation is difficult to detect if the entire network shuts down.

Because the fault may lie anywhere along the shared line and no central device monitors each connection, engineers usually test the cable segment by segment to find the exact break, which is a slow, manual process with few shortcuts.

Disadvantage 4 of 7
d

One Building Only

Textbook lineWhen the network is required in more than one building, the bus network cannot be used.

A single backbone cable has practical limits on length and signal loss, so extending one bus between separate buildings is not realistic. Networks spanning several buildings require a different design, such as an extended star.

Disadvantage 5 of 7
e

Signal Weakens With Nodes

Textbook lineThe signal becomes weaker if the number of nodes increases.

Each extra device tapped onto the bus draws a small amount of energy from the shared signal, and every added junction causes some loss. The more nodes join the line, the weaker the signal is when it reaches the far end.

Disadvantage 6 of 7
f

Slower as Nodes Increase

Textbook linePerformance degradation occurs with the increased number of nodes.

All devices compete for the same limited bandwidth on one cable, so as more machines join, each receives a smaller share and must wait longer for a free turn to transmit. As a result, the network becomes slower.

Disadvantage 7 of 7
g

Data Collisions

Textbook lineCollision of data can take place as several nodes can transmit data to each other at a time.

Because the cable is a shared medium, two devices may begin sending at the same instant, and their signals collide and corrupt each other. Both must then wait and retransmit, which wastes time and lowers effective throughput.
